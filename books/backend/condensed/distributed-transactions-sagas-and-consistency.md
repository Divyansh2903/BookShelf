# Distributed Transactions, Sagas, and Consistency

Part 5 covered how services own and share data day to day. This part covers one business operation that must update data across multiple services with no shared database transaction.

## Why ACID is not enough

A database transaction gives atomicity, consistency, isolation, and durability within one database. Once data is split across service databases, that guarantee stops at the service boundary: no single engine coordinates a commit across them, so a transaction can't span them. Distributed approaches must work around this.

## Two-phase commit

The direct attempt to recreate ACID across services: a coordinator asks every participant to prepare, waits for all to confirm they can commit, then tells everyone to commit.

It guarantees atomicity, but:

- Every participant holds locks and resources while waiting on the others (blocking).
- If the coordinator or any participant is slow or down, everyone waits (latency and availability problems).
- In microservices that deploy and scale independently, this tight coupling defeats the point of splitting them. It's rarely the right tool.

## Saga pattern

A **saga** is a sequence of local transactions, each committed independently in its own service, with a defined compensating action per step in case a later step fails.

Instead of one transaction that fully commits or rolls back, you get small independently committed transactions plus a plan to undo earlier steps. It trades strict atomicity for something that works across independent services.

## Compensating transactions

A new transaction that semantically reverses a committed step. It's not a rollback: the original step already committed and is visible. E.g. if a payment was charged and a later step fails, the compensation is "refund the payment," not "pretend the charge never happened."

This matters because other parts of the system may have reacted to the committed step before compensation runs, and the design must account for that.

## Orchestration

A central coordinator tells each service what to do next and tracks workflow state. Complex workflows are easier to understand and debug since the whole sequence lives in one place. Tradeoff: every participating service is coupled to the orchestrator.

## Choreography

No coordinator: each service reacts to events published by others and decides its next action. Services are more loosely coupled since none knows the whole workflow. Works for simple flows with few steps, but gets hard to reason about as steps grow, with no single place showing the full sequence.

## Transactional outbox

Problem: committing a database change and publishing an event are two actions. A crash between them leaves a committed change with no event, or an event with no committed change.

Fix: write the event to an **outbox table** in the same transaction as the business data, so both succeed or fail together. A separate relay process later reads the outbox and publishes. Write and publish are decoupled in time but not in correctness.

## Inbox pattern

The consumer-side counterpart: the consumer tracks IDs of processed events, so a duplicate delivery is recognized and skipped instead of applied twice.

## Idempotency keys

A unique key per operation, usually generated by the caller and sent with the request. If the server has seen the key, it returns the stored result instead of re-executing. This makes retrying operations like "charge this payment" safe from duplicate side effects.

## At-least-once delivery

Most messaging systems default to at-least-once: a message may arrive more than once but always arrives. Combined with idempotent consumers (inbox pattern or idempotency keys), you get effectively exactly-once behavior at the application level despite the weaker underlying guarantee.

## Dead-letter queues

Messages that keep failing after retries go to a **dead-letter queue** instead of being retried forever or silently dropped. There they can be inspected, fixed, and replayed manually without blocking the rest of the queue.

## Exponential backoff with jitter

Immediate repeated retries can cause a **retry storm**: many clients hammering a struggling service and slowing its recovery. **Exponential backoff** increases the delay between attempts; **jitter** randomizes it so clients don't retry in lockstep. Together they spread retries out instead of concentrating them when the dependency is least able to cope.

## Partial failure

Handle saga failures by type:

- **Transient** errors (e.g. a brief network blip): retry.
- **Permanent** errors (e.g. a validation failure that will never succeed): run a compensating action.
- **Failed compensation**: escalate, don't fail silently; you likely have inconsistent state needing a human.

## State machines

A saga is a state machine: defined valid states and valid transitions. Modeling it explicitly, instead of loose flags and conditionals, lets you reject invalid transitions outright (e.g. refusing to ship an order never marked paid) rather than discovering inconsistency later.

## Human intervention

Some failures can't or shouldn't be resolved automatically, especially those involving money or irreversible external side effects; a person must decide. Tooling for this (dashboards of stuck sagas, clear context on current state and what's been tried) is as much part of the system as automated retry and compensation. Treating it as an afterthought is how stuck sagas sit unresolved for days.
